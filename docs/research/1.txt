Abstract
“Computer Organization and Design” is a core foundational course
for computer science majors. With its abstract content, complex
structure, and close integration of theory and practice, students
often face challenges such as difficulty understanding concepts,
insufficient knowledge transfer, low efficiency in experimental de-
bugging, and inconsistent engagement with the course. This paper
explores the impact of student learning behaviors on learning out-
comes, focusing on both the theoretical and practical aspects of
the course. Based on educational theories and the characteristics
of the computing discipline, this paper constructs a framework
for analyzing learning behaviors to examine students’ cognitive
levels, thinking patterns, personality traits, and behavioral char-
acteristics. By collecting survey feedback, student learning out-
comes, and interview data, learning behavior indicators were de-
veloped, and a mixed-methods approach was used to study student
learning behaviors. The results indicate that high engagement in
higher-order cognitive training, computational thinking training,
hardware thinking training, critical thinking training, and positive
personality traits are all significantly correlated with course grades,
practical performance, and research literacy. The study also found
that some students exhibited issues such as fragmented conceptual
understanding, inappropriate application of artificial intelligence,
insufficient practical training time, and low-quality reflection. This

Problem Statement
“Computer Organization and Design” is a core undergraduate com-
puter science course that connects prior and subsequent learning.
It covers digital logic, data representation, arithmetic and control
units, memory systems, instruction sets, and CPU architecture, pro-
viding a foundation for understanding computer operation from
hardware to system level and for later courses such as Operating
Systems, Computer Architecture, Embedded Systems, and Com-
piler Principles. It also helps develop systems thinking, engineering
awareness, and problem-analysis ability. The course is challeng-
ing because it contains abstract concepts, a complex structure, and
strong integration of theory and practice. Topics such as timing con-
trol, micro-operations, pipelining, and memory hierarchy involve

multi-level mechanisms, making it difficult for students to build a
coherent understanding. Students must master both low-level hard-
ware logic and system-level coordination, while laboratory work
requires circuit design, fault diagnosis, result analysis, and report
writing, placing high demands on knowledge transfer, practical
skills, and reflection. In the AI era and new engineering context, the
course goal has expanded from knowledge delivery to cultivating
computational thinking, engineering thinking, critical thinking,
hardware systems thinking, and early research literacy[7]. Scien-

hypotheses, interpreting results, and refining solutions. Since de-
bugging, performance comparison, design optimization, and error
analysis already involve research elements, studying student be-
havior in theory and practice can improve teaching and support
the development of scientific literacy. Platform-generated learning
data also enables behavior analysis and personalized intervention.
Despite ongoing reform, problems remain: fragmented knowledge,
weak theory–practice integration, uneven engagement, misuse of
AI tools, and result-oriented assessment. Based on this, this paper
focuses on three research questions: RQ1: Is student engagement
in higher-order cognitive practice tasks positively correlated with
course grades and subsequent research literacy performance in
advanced experiments? RQ2: Is student engagement in computa-
tional thinking, hardware thinking, and critical thinking training
tasks positively correlated with learning outcomes and research
literacy? RQ3: Are positive student personality traits and habits
positively correlated with learning outcomes and research literacy
performance?

Background and related work
This study is grounded in scholarship on learning behavior analy-
sis, online learning, computer science education, and the teaching
of “Computer Organization and Design.” With the growth of dig-
ital education, learning behavior analysis has become central to
learning sciences and higher education because it uses platform,
experimental, and interaction data to identify patterns relevant
to teaching improvement and competency development [13, 26].
This perspective is especially valuable in computer systems courses,
where students must understand abstract topics such as processor
organization, memory hierarchy, and performance optimization,
while also developing research literacy through experimentation,
analysis, and academic communication.
The theoretical basis of this study is multidisciplinary. Construc-
tivism argues that knowledge is actively built through exploration,
interaction, and reflection [22, 28]; thus, behaviors such as simula-
tion, debugging, and optimization can be interpreted as external
manifestations of internal cognition. Self-regulated learning the-
ory further highlights goal setting, strategy use, monitoring, and
reflection as key drivers of learning outcomes [23, 36], often re-
flected in indicators such as task initiation, pacing, resource use,
and revision frequency [31]. Deep learning theory distinguishes
meaningful understanding and transfer from surface memoriza-
tion [19], with deeper engagement typically linked to stronger
outcomes [8]. Social constructivism and situated learning also em-
phasize the role of collaboration, engagement, and feedback within
communities of practice [17, 28], making interactional behaviors
an important analytic dimension [32]. Collectively, these theories
suggest that competency development is dynamic and partially
observable through behavioral data [6, 15].
Prior research has shown that online learning behaviors such
as login frequency, resource access, assignment submission, and
forum participation are associated with grades, persistence, and
engagement [25, 26, 34]. Methodologically, the field has progressed
from descriptive statistics to clustering, sequence analysis, social
network analysis, and process mining [16, 20]. In computer science
education, research has increasingly used learning management
systems and programming platforms to model learners, predict risk,
and support intervention [3, 18, 25, 29]. Chinese studies have simi-
larly examined blended and online environments using classroom,
MOOC, and Online Judge data [9, 33]. However, many studies still
rely on surface-level frequency indicators and insufficiently capture
code quality, problem-solving processes, higher-order cognition, or
research-oriented competencies [13, 15].
Research on “Computer Organization and Design” has mainly
focused on curriculum design, laboratory reform, blended teaching,
and assessment. Internationally, Computer Science Curricula 2013
emphasizes abstract thinking, hardware–software coordination,
and system design [2], while many institutions employ simula-
tors, FPGA-based labs, visual tools, and project-based learning [21].
Active learning and flipped classrooms have also been shown to im-
prove understanding of abstract hardware concepts [24]. In China,
related studies have emphasized integrated knowledge structures,
design-oriented experiments, and blended models combining online
learning, seminars, and laboratory practice [5, 12, 27].
Despite these advances, important gaps remain. Existing studies
of learning behavior rarely target “Computer Organization and
Design,” a course that uniquely integrates hardware theory and ex-
perimental practice. Moreover, prior work has paid limited attention
to research literacy, reflective activity, theory–practice integration,
and higher-order cognition. To address these gaps, this study pro-
poses a course-specific learning behavior analysis framework based
on multi-source data, including platform records, questionnaires,
observations, interviews, grades, and lab reports. It examines practi-
cal engagement, interaction, reflection, individual traits, and behav-
ioral habits, with particular emphasis on higher-order cognition,
computational thinking, hardware thinking, and critical thinking,
to clarify their relationships with learning outcomes and inform
instructional improvement.

Research Methods
3.1
Research Participants and Data Sources
This study examines 110 undergraduate computer science majors at
a particular university who were enrolled in the course “Computer
Organization and Design” [14, 35]. The research covers two main
components of the course: theoretical instruction and laboratory
practice. To enhance the reliability of the research findings, this
study collected data from multiple sources to create a comprehen-
sive profile of the learning process. First, questionnaire survey data.
A questionnaire was designed around dimensions such as students’
cognitive engagement, learning strategies, preparation for experi-
ments, reflection habits, use of AI tools, willingness to participate
in classroom interactions, self-efficacy, and self-assessments of re-
search literacy to understand students’ subjective perceptions and

learning behaviors of students in the “Computer Organization and
Design” course from as many perspectives as possible.

Given that the “Computer Organization and Design” course com-
bines both theoretical study and practical laboratory work, this
paper focuses on three aspects when designing student learning
behavior indicators: higher-order cognitive levels, thinking skills,
and personal habits.

3.2.1
Cognitive Level Indicators. Bloom’s taxonomy of higher-order
cognitive levels[4] includes analysis, evaluation, and creation. The
practical sessions for “Computer Organization and Design” are not
limited to the lower-level “remembering and understanding” but
cover the higher-order objectives in Bloom’s cognitive theory: (1)
Application: Students apply theoretical knowledge to Verilog model-
ing, processor implementation, peripheral interfacing, and software
development. (2)Analysis: Students must decompose instruction ex-
ecution processes, hardware structural relationships, interface tim-
ing, and sources of system performance.(3)Evaluation: Students as-
sess design correctness and performance through simulation, bench-
marks, performance counters, and debugging results.(4)Creation:
Students will ultimately be able to independently complete system-
level designs and functional extensions for processors, SoCs, caches,
DMAs, and other components. Table 1 lists specific indicators for
students’ performance on higher-order cognitive tasks in the “Com-
puter Organization and Design” course.
3.2.2
Indicators of Thinking Skills. The cognitive abilities discussed
in this paper primarily include computational thinking[11, 30], hard-
ware (language) thinking, and critical thinking[10]. These three
modes of thinking are not isolated from one another but are inter-
twined and mutually reinforcing. Computational thinking encom-
passes abstraction, decomposition, algorithms, and pattern recogni-
tion. It emphasizes “how to transform complex systems into prob-
lems that are understandable, implementable, and verifiable,” and
is therefore primarily manifested in module partitioning, instruc-
tion analysis, program design, and performance analysis. Hardware
thinking encompasses timing and state awareness, debugging and
verification, and parallel processing and resource trade-offs. It em-
phasizes “how hardware systems actually operate under clock, state,
interface, and resource constraints,” and is therefore primarily re-
flected in CPU implementation, state machine design, handshake
timing, integration testing and verification, and SoC scaling. Crit-
ical thinking is a cyclical training process comprising four steps:
observation, questioning, verification, and revision as shown in
Figure 2. Through this process, students will develop a coherent
methodology: not taking conclusions at face value, actively verify-
ing hypotheses, distinguishing facts from interpretations, paying
attention to boundary conditions, comparing alternatives, and iden-
tifying the strength of evidence [34]. Critical thinking is the most
fundamental component of scientific literacy. It emphasizes “how
to continuously approach correct conclusions through the process
of observation—questioning—verification—revision”; therefore, it
permeates the entire experimental process and serves as the core
method for ensuring design quality and fostering scientific literacy.
Among these, cultivating hardware (language) thinking presents
certain challenges and requires reinforcement through practice.
Building on foundational computational thinking skills, this paper
primarily focuses on enhancing students’ research literacy by cul-
tivating their critical thinking abilities, without addressing other
aspects of research literacy.

3.2.3
Personality and Habit Indicators. When evaluating the “Com-
puter Organization and Design” course, it is necessary to incorpo-
rate personality-related learning behavior indicators such as adapt-
able, inventive, meticulous, self-directed, and passionate[1]. These
indicators help assess students’ learning performance and skill de-
velopment in this course more comprehensively and scientifically,
while also guiding them to adopt learning methods suited to the
course’s characteristics. Table 3 presents the behavioral indicators
directly used in classroom observations, formative assessments, and
periodic summaries. The inclusion of adaptable indicators is primar-
ily based on the course’s multi-layered, broad scope, which requires
students to flexibly switch among abstract theory, logical analysis,
and structural design. The inclusion of inventive indicators stems
from the course’s strong emphasis on synthesis and design. Tasks
often lack a single optimal solution; evaluating students’ ability to
propose multiple solutions and compare their merits helps measure
their innovative thinking and engineering design capabilities. The
inclusion of meticulous indicators stems from the high-precision
requirements of hardware systems regarding logical relationships
and timing control. Therefore, attention must be paid to students’
adherence to standards and accuracy during practical work. The
inclusion of self-directed learning indicators is due to the course’s
extreme difficulty, as classroom instruction cannot cover the entire
learning process. Students’ ability to proactively prepare, orga-
nize knowledge, conduct independent experiments, and engage in
timely reflection will directly impact their learning outcomes. The
inclusion of passionate indicators is intended to reflect that sus-
tained interest is a key factor in helping students overcome learning
difficulties, maintain engagement, and persist in exploration.
3.3
Research Methods
This paper is a preliminary exploratory case study that employs a
mixed-methods approach combining quantitative and qualitative
methods.
3.3.1
Mixed-Methods Research. Because a single method cannot
fully capture students’ personality traits and behavioral habits, this
study adopts a mixed qualitative–quantitative approach. Qualita-
tive analysis examines motivations, attitudes, emotions, and cogni-
tive processes behind behavior through questionnaires, classroom
and lab observations, and follow-up interviews. It helps explain
differences in learning interest, self-management, collaboration, re-
silience, concentration, peer interaction, and responses to difficulty,
failure, and feedback. Quantitative analysis provides comparable
evidence through indicators such as task completion time, revision
frequency, submission counts, work quality, error rates, and respon-
siveness to feedback, reflecting engagement, efficiency, persistence,
reflection, and planning. Combining the two approaches improves
both reliability and explanatory power, thereby supporting targeted
teaching interventions.
To gather such evidence, this study uses a questionnaire in the
first half of the “Computer Organization and Design” course, tar-
geting students enrolled in the theory course and/or laboratory
seminar. The survey investigates the implementation of teaching
and learning experiences, platform support, and course improve-
ment needs, aiming to capture authentic student perceptions and
practical demands. The questionnaire consists of 72 questions cover-
ing eight dimensions: 1. Basic student and enrollment information;
2. Course learning experience, including perceived difficulty, pace,
and ability to follow; 3. Learning engagement, measured by study
time for theory and laboratory tasks; 4. Experience with the ex-
perimental platform and related tools; 5. Evaluation of classroom
instruction and assessment sessions; 6. Difficulties in experimental
learning, such as theory, Verilog, debugging, hardware thinking,
Git/GitLab, CI/CD tools, and platform access; 7. Learning support
methods and AI tool use in help-seeking, knowledge retrieval, code
generation, debugging, and report writing; 8. Teacher–student in-
teraction and broader learning development, including interaction
preferences, interest changes, behavioral characteristics, satisfac-
tion, grade expectations, career support, and suggestions.

Qualitative Analysis. Qualitative analysis methods are used

4
Analysis of Preliminary Results
The questionnaire response rate was 96.4%, with a 100% comple-
tion rate; there were no instances of perfunctory responses, dupli-
cate submissions, or dropouts, indicating high data quality. Table
4 shows that the overall reliability of the questionnaire is good.
Preliminary analysis shows differences in students’ learning behav-
iors, particularly evident in laboratory practice. High-performing
students were able to flexibly apply concepts, principles, and tools,
identify key checkpoints, and adopt systematic, hardware-oriented
debugging strategies; in contrast, lower-performing students relied
more on blind trial and error and lacked the ability to pinpoint errors
and reflect on their processes. Overall, students generally lacked
systematic reflection; they rarely reviewed experimental steps, cat-
egorized errors, compared approaches, or proposed improvements.
High-quality reflection was more common among students with
stronger academic performance and research literacy. Taking Lab3
as an example (see Table 2 in Section 3.2.2), Figure 4 shows that
students spent relatively little time on average during the design
phase (3.6 hours) but achieved high completion rates for functional
block diagrams and module division, indicating that they placed sig-
nificant emphasis on this phase. The development phase averaged
6.9 hours, with noticeable variations in code quality: some code
lacked comments or simulation waveform screenshots, reflecting
insufficient training in hardware-oriented thinking among some
students. The debugging phase took the longest on average (8.4
hours), indicating that debugging was the most time-consuming

Preliminary evidence suggests that high-quality learning behav-
iors are positively correlated with academic performance and re-
search literacy. Students who engage in higher-order practical tasks,
computational thinking, hardware thinking, and critical thinking
activities typically achieve better grades and demonstrate stronger
research literacy in subsequent advanced experiments (RQ1, RQ2).
Surveys and interviews also show that positive personal traits are
positively correlated with academic performance and research lit-
eracy (RQ3). This suggests that it is the quality of learning behav-
iors—rather than mere task completion—that truly influences deep
learning and long-term development. The analysis also revealed
some persistent issues in student learning: some students’ knowl-
edge construction is fragmented, making it difficult for them to
understand the connections between data representation, instruc-
tion execution, memory levels, and component coordination; AI
tools are often used to obtain answers, code, or step-by-step in-
structions, rather than for analysis, verification, and reflection(as
shown in Figure 5); some students do not invest sufficient effort in
experiment preparation, debugging, and summarization, and their
reflections often remain at the level of operational descriptions.
In response, instructors should provide stronger guidance and of-
fer personalized support to students whose learning outcomes are
limited by their personal traits.
Based on the above findings, mechanisms that promote effective
learning behaviors can be incorporated into instructional design to
guide students toward shifting their focus from “completing tasks”
to “understanding principles, testing hypotheses, and reflecting on
and improving their work.”(1) Problem-based learning can promote

students’ in-depth understanding of theoretical knowledge, thereby
enhancing their cognitive levels and thinking skills, particularly
critical thinking skills. (2) Strengthen training in experimental de-
bugging and reflective skills. By introducing debugging logs, error
tracking sheets, and process reflection forms to optimize the de-
sign of experimental platforms, we can promote the internalization
of these behaviors and foster stable yet flexible thinking habits.
(3) Regulate and guide the use of AI-assisted learning. Courses
should clearly define the boundaries of its use, encourage students
to use it for comparing concepts, troubleshooting, organizing their
thoughts, and retrieving information, and require them to verify the
basis for the output. Teachers can design “AI Response Analysis”
assignments to cultivate students’ ability to use AI critically.
5
Contributions and Future Work
The innovation of this paper lies in proposing a framework for
analyzing learning behaviors in the “Computer Organization and
Design” course that integrates cognitive and thinking levels with
personality traits. This framework provides a reference for differ-
entiated instruction, formative assessment, the design of digital
practice platforms, and personalized learning support. In the future,
the authors plan to expand the existing framework to include di-
mensions of research literacy—such as scientific methods, research
design, and data literacy—and to enrich the digital educational
elements within the practical platform to enhance the overall effec-
tiveness of course instruction and practical support.
